Pompeii Walkabout

Investigate what a city can reveal through buildings, objects, writing and human remains. Pompeii is a remarkable source, but it is one town frozen during one disaster—not a picture of every Roman life.

1. Eight evidence stops

Choose the most useful main category for each find: building, object, writing or human remains. Some finds could fit more than one category; explain your choice.

Stop 1

Street-side food counter

A stone counter opens on to the street. Large jars are set into it; archaeologists recorded about 150 similar counters in town.

Stop 2

Stepping stones and cart ruts

Raised stones cross a paved street. Gaps allow carts to pass, and grooves worn into the stone show repeated traffic.

Stop 3

Election notice

A painted wall asks people to elect Marcus Holconius Priscus. The notice connects public writing with local politics.

Stop 4

A name and a date

A scratched wall names Gaius Pumidius Dipilus and gives a date equivalent to 78 BCE.

Stop 5

Loaves in an oven

Eighty-one round loaves were found blackened inside a baker's oven.

Stop 6

“Beware of the dog”

A floor mosaic at a doorway shows a chained dog and a warning to visitors.

Stop 7

House, garden and shops

A large home had a garden and wall paintings, with small street-facing shops and sleeping space above them.

Stop 8

Shapes in the ash

Hollow spaces in hardened ash were filled with plaster, preserving the shapes of people and a dog.

The cast shapes are evidence of people caught in the eruption, but they do not tell us every person's identity or thoughts. A category is a starting point; the explanation matters.

2. Read an eyewitness account

Pliny the Younger described a cloud rising above Vesuvius like a tall trunk that spread into branches. He was about seventeen and watched from Misenum, roughly 30 km away. He wrote to the historian Tacitus about 25 years later.

Paraphrase of Pliny, Letters 6.16. Pliny's letters are evidence from a witness, but he was not standing in Pompeii.
