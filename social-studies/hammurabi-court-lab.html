Interactive evidence investigation

Hammurabi Court Lab

Predict a verdict, reveal the source, and investigate a difficult question: did written law create order, fairness, or both?

Learning goal: Use at least two laws as evidence for a claim about order and fairness. The point is your reasoning, not guessing a single “right” opinion.

The English versions below are historical translations. They are not modern legal advice or a full record of how every court case was decided. Student writing stays in this page and is not submitted or saved online.

Phase 1 · Predict before the evidence

You be the judge

For each case, make a quick prediction first. Write a few words or think it through, then reveal what the surviving law says. You can disagree with the law and explain why.

Case A · The unproved accusation

A person accuses a neighbour of a serious crime but cannot prove it.

Law 1
“If a man bring an accusation against a man, and charge him with a (capital) crime, but cannot prove it, he, the accuser, shall be put to death.”

Translated by Robert F. Harper, 1904.

The law gives a severe penalty for an unproved accusation. What might it have been meant to discourage? The text alone cannot tell us how often this sentence was carried out.

Case B · The fallen house

A builder works carelessly. The house collapses and kills its owner.

Law 229
“If a builder build a house for some one, and does not construct it properly, and the house which he built fall in and kill its owner, then that builder shall be put to death.”

Translated by L. W. King, 1910.

The twist · Law 230

If the collapse kills the owner’s son, the law says the builder’s son is put to death. Who is punished here? What does this reveal about how the Code understood responsibility?

Law 230 · L. W. King, 1910.

Case C · The failed surgery

A doctor uses a bronze knife in surgery. The patient dies.

Law 218
“If a physician make a large incision with the operating knife, and kill him … his hands shall be cut off.”

Translated by L. W. King, 1910. The ellipsis marks omitted words.

What did this law expect from professional work? What information about intent, training or the full trial is missing from the line?

Phase 2 · Compare the consequences

One injury, three punishments

All three laws describe an attacker putting out someone’s eye. Change only the victim’s social class and compare what happens.

Law 196

Free landowner

The attacker’s own eye is put out.

The punishment is bodily retaliation; no payment is listed.

Law 198

Commoner

The attacker pays one mina of silver.

Older translations call this person a “freed man.”

Law 199

Enslaved person

The attacker pays half the person’s price.

The law says payment goes to the enslaved person’s owner.

Compare: What stayed the same across the three laws? What changed? In Law 199, who receives the payment?

Source check: The Code’s prologue says Hammurabi’s laws should stop “the strong” from harming “the weak.” How does that claim sit beside these three different outcomes?

Phase 3 · Make an evidence-based claim

Order, fairness, or both?

Choose a position and at least two pieces of evidence. Use the generated scaffold to organize your own explanation.
