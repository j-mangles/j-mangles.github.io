Interactive source investigation

Sargon’s Empire Evidence Lab

Two stories tell how Sargon became king. A victory monument tells how his grandson wanted to be seen. Investigate what each source can—and cannot—show.

Investigation question: Which account of Sargon’s rise is more useful to a historian, and what limits should the historian keep in mind?

Neither account was written during Sargon’s lifetime. A source can tell us what people said or wanted others to believe without proving every detail happened exactly as described. Student writing stays on this page and is not sent or saved online.

Account 1 · Legend

A birth chosen by the gods

The story says Sargon’s mother hid him in a basket on the river. Akki rescued him and raised him as a gardener. The goddess Ishtar then chose him to become king.

What it emphasizes: a humble beginning, rescue and divine support.

The ancient text survives in much later copies. Translators disagree about one description of Sargon’s mother.

Account 2 · King List

A palace servant takes power

The King List says Sargon was cupbearer to Ur-Zababa, king of Kish. He later became king and built the city of Akkad.

What it emphasizes: a palace role and a political rise.

The list was compiled long after Sargon and recopied. Some copies give different lengths for his reign.

Historian’s move: Ask who made each account, when it was written down, what it claims, and what problem remains—even for the source you find more useful.

Phase 2 · Check what the evidence supports

Source claim or established fact?

Choose the strongest reading for each statement. “A source says this” and “this definitely happened” are different conclusions.

The King List says Sargon served Ur-Zababa before taking power.

Ishtar chose Sargon to rule.

Archaeologists know exactly where the city of Akkad stood.

The victory stele proves the gods approved Naram-Sin’s campaign.

The famous bronze head definitely shows Sargon rather than Naram-Sin.

Phase 3 · Read the monument

What is the stone trying to make you believe?

Naram-Sin, Sargon’s grandson, had a victory stele carved. This simplified diagram highlights details described in the lesson; it is not a picture of the actual artifact.

★ Star symbols: divine presence♟ Large horned king: power and godlike status↓ Smaller enemies: defeat

The monument makes a powerful royal argument: the king is larger than other people, wears horns associated with gods, and defeats enemies while divine symbols appear above him. That is evidence of the message Naram-Sin wanted to send. It cannot prove the gods caused the victory or that every event unfolded exactly as shown.
