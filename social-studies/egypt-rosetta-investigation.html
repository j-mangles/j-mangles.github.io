Unit 2 · Ancient Egypt · Lesson 12

The Rosetta Stone Investigation

← Egypt atlas

For about 1,400 years, nobody could read hieroglyphs. Investigate how scholars cracked the code, separate a documented death from the “mummy’s curse,” and weigh a question about who should hold an ancient object.

01 · CRACK THE CODE

Two royal names, one sound key

Scholars could read the Greek on the Rosetta Stone. It named Ptolemy V. A royal name sits inside a cartouche, and foreign names had to be spelled by sound. Follow the sign labels from the student assignment.

Cartouche A

𓊪 𓏏 𓍯 𓃭 𓅓 𓇌 𓋴

stool · loaf · lasso · lion · owl · two reeds · cloth

Cartouche B

𓈎 𓃭 𓇋 𓍯 𓊪 𓄿 𓂧 𓂋 𓄿

hill · lion · reed · lasso · stool · vulture · hand · mouth · vulture

Why was this proof? The same signs appeared in both royal names and represented the same sounds. That suggested hieroglyphs could record sounds, not only whole ideas or pictures. Champollion’s knowledge of Coptic—the last living form of Egyptian—helped him read the language behind the signs.

02 · PROVEN, REASONABLE GUESS OR LEGEND?

Check the “mummy’s curse” story

Classify each statement. A real event can get linked to an explanation that the evidence does not prove.

Lord Carnarvon died in April 1923, about five months after the tomb was opened.

An ancient curse caused Carnarvon’s death.

A curse was carved over Tutankhamun’s tomb door.

Newspapers repeated curse stories because dramatic stories could attract readers.

03 · WEIGH THE EVIDENCE

Should the Rosetta Stone be returned to Egypt?

The stone was found in Egypt by a French soldier in 1799, taken by the British in 1801, and has been in the British Museum since 1802. There are arguments on both sides; make your own case and say what evidence would strengthen it.

Reasons to return it

It was made and found in Egypt. Returning it could recognize Egyptian history and give Egyptian communities greater access to their heritage.

Reasons to keep it in London

It has been displayed in the British Museum for generations and is part of a large public collection. Keeping it there can support study and public access, if the museum provides meaningful interpretation and access.
